    How nolainocr is built

    This chapter explains the product bet, why responsibilities are split across four layers, and which invariants and trade-offs follow. The next chapter names the components; the workflow chapter owns the page-by-page journey.

    You do not need to know the codebase. By the end you will have a small vocabulary — page, session, schema, template group, page quota — and a picture of four layers that everything else hangs on.

    The core bet

    Imagine a folder of supplier invoices. You want a spreadsheet of invoice numbers, dates, totals, and line items. You do not want to type them. You also do not want a tool that only works if every vendor used the same PDF generator.

    nolainocr’s bet is: same layout, structured extraction.

    You upload pages that share a template. The service reads the first page well enough to name the fields — that list of names is the schema — then reads the rest of the batch into those columns. Labels that mean the same thing in different words are merged so the table stays rectangular enough to export.

    The analogy is a clerk and a stack of identical forms. You do not hire someone to invent a new filing system per sheet. You show one filled example; the clerk copies the boxes onto every following page. Mixed forms in one stack defeat the clerk. Two stacks, two runs, two clean tables.

    That constraint is the product. Everything below is how the constraint is kept reliable, cheap enough, and hard to misuse.

    Four layers

    Think of four floors, from closest to you to farthest from the document pixels.

    Experience layer      the website: landing, dashboard, account, tools, export
Orchestration layer   accepts uploads, enforces limits, runs jobs, reports progress
Extraction layer      turns pages into structured records and aligns columns
Settlement layer      who you are, which plan you are on, what the pages cost

    The document plane is experience + orchestration + extraction: take pages, return a table. The control plane is settlement: identity, quota, payment. Uploaded files never become a long-lived warehouse; they belong to a session and leave when you log out.

    Experience layer

    The experience layer is nolainocr.com: marketing and solutions pages, sign-in, the dashboard, account and billing screens, the blog, and the free browser tools.

    It does not read document pixels, charge pages, or decide schema. This keeps the browser a client of the service, while accepting that a multi-page job cannot finish inside one short HTTP request — progress has to come back to the screen.

    Orchestration layer

    The orchestration layer treats an upload as a job, not as an unbounded chat. It checks that the file type and page count are allowed, stores pages for the session, starts extraction, and streams progress until a table is ready.

    It does not invent field names or talk to the card network. The gate saves wasted model calls when a plan is exhausted, but it means a preview is not yet a billable page — only a finished extraction is.

    Extraction layer

    Once a job is admitted, the extraction layer turns each page into structured data. The first page proposes the schema. Later pages are read into that shape. Nested values are flattened into spreadsheet columns. Names that are close in meaning are unified so “Ref.” and “Invoice No.” do not become two sparse columns.

    It does not own identity or prices. Quality depends on scan quality and on the same-layout rule; a mixed stack is out of scope, not a prompt to be clever.

    Settlement layer

    The settlement layer identifies the caller, exposes plan limits, and charges pages when extraction completes. Monthly quota is consumed before top-up pages. Card data is collected by the payment provider, not by this layer’s document path.

    Charging by completed page follows useful work, so an abandoned upload does not spend the month. Two similar-looking PDFs can still differ in page count, and therefore in cost.

    Durable invariants

    The implementation can evolve without changing the product’s identity only while these boundaries remain true.

    Documents are session-scoped

    • Uploads exist to be extracted, not to be archived.
    • Logout deletes the working files.
    • Exports you download are yours; they are not a second copy the service keeps as a library.

    Because extraction must read pixels, documents do leave your laptop for the duration of the session. That is data minimization in time, not a claim that pages never transit the service. The legal detail is Data and privacy.

    Layout is a batch contract

    • One run assumes one template.
    • A template group (1–4 consecutive pages) is how a repeating multi-page document is described, not a way to mix unrelated forms.
    • The schema is learned from the start of the batch and applied forward.

    If a change requires the extractor to classify an arbitrary mixed pile in one click, it is fighting this design.

    Pages are the unit of settlement

    • Limits are pages per document and pages per month.
    • A page is counted when extraction finishes.
    • Quota before top-up; refusal when both are empty.

    Payments stay at the payment provider

    • Plan and usage records live with the account.
    • Card numbers do not.

    Trade-offs that follow

    Same-layout batches over mixed-document intelligence. The table is cleaner, and the product is honest about when to split a folder.

    Session files over a document CMS. Disk and retention stay small; users must keep originals themselves.

    Async jobs over one blocking request. Large stacks can finish without the browser timing out; the dashboard must stay open enough to receive progress.

    Semantic column alignment over exact string matching. “Total” and “Amount due” can merge; occasional over-merging is the cost.

    Hosted vision over local classical OCR. Structured JSON and language coverage come with vendor cost and rate limits. The design choices chapter names that bet without turning it into a vendor scorecard.
